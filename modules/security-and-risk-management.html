Module Overview

Understanding Security and Risk Management

Security and risk are increasingly interconnected within modern organisations. As enterprises adopt increasingly integrated digital systems, cybersecurity risk must be considered alongside wider operational, financial, regulatory and strategic risks.

This module develops an understanding of the methods used to identify, analyse, evaluate and treat cybersecurity risks. It considers both qualitative and quantitative risk assessment approaches and examines how different methods may be appropriate in different organisational contexts.

The module also explores established threat modelling approaches including STRIDE, DREAD, Attack Trees and PASTA, together with tools and resources that support threat analysis and vulnerability prioritisation.

Additional areas of study include security and regulatory standards, quantitative risk modelling, business continuity, disaster recovery and emerging trends within the wider field of security and risk management.

Units 3–4

Threat Modelling and Management

Units 3 and 4 develop the distinction between threats and vulnerabilities and introduce established methods for representing potential attacks and prioritising security risks.

01

STRIDE

A structured technique for identifying threats involving spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege.

02

DREAD

A risk-rating approach traditionally used to assess and prioritise the severity of identified threats.

03

Attack Trees

A graphical technique for representing an attacker's primary objective and the possible pathways through which that objective may be achieved.

04

PASTA

A risk-centred threat modelling methodology designed to connect business objectives, technical analysis and possible attack scenarios.

05

MITRE ATT&CK

A knowledge base that supports analysis of adversary tactics, techniques and behaviour during threat modelling.

06

OWASP and CVSS

Resources that assist with vulnerability understanding, threat analysis and prioritisation.

My Reflection

Threat modelling has strengthened my understanding that identifying vulnerabilities alone is not sufficient. Effective security analysis must also consider attack objectives, likely pathways, dependencies, attacker behaviour and possible consequences.

Units 7–8

Quantitative Risk Modelling

These units move beyond qualitative assessment and introduce mathematical, probabilistic and multi-criteria approaches to representing uncertainty and risk.

01

Monte Carlo Simulation

Monte Carlo methods use repeated simulations to represent uncertainty and estimate the distribution of possible outcomes.

02

Bayesian Methods

Bayesian approaches use probability and available evidence to update estimates of uncertainty and risk.

03

Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis

Techniques such as TOPSIS, AHP and ANP support decision-making where several competing criteria influence the final risk-management decision.

Identifying critical organisational functions and evaluating the consequences of disruption.

Recovery Time Objective

Defining the target period within which a disrupted service or system should be restored.

Defining the acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time, following disruption.

Selecting technical and organisational recovery arrangements that meet defined recovery requirements.

Considering redundancy, vendor lock-in, network security and other resilience factors within disaster recovery planning.

Assessment Evidence

Final Critical Reflection

Integrated final reflection using What? So what? Now what? (Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper, 2001). Reflective text: approximately 601 words; confirm the final count before submission.

What?

Studying Security and Risk Management broadened my understanding of cybersecurity beyond network faults and technical vulnerabilities. As a fibre-optic engineer, I previously approached reliability primarily through infrastructure, connectivity and troubleshooting. The module introduced qualitative and quantitative assessment, threat modelling, governance, legal obligations, business continuity and disaster recovery. These topics showed me why security decisions must reflect organisational priorities as well as technical exposure. In the Pampered Pets team project, I was assigned the role of Chief Editor, Operations Manager and Visual Designer. My responsibilities included drafting a concise introduction and conclusion, integrating contributions, controlling the 1,000-word limit, preparing a timeline or Gantt chart, and checking Harvard references, including the required Hancock et al. (2024) source.

So what?

My most important conceptual change was recognising that a vulnerability is not automatically an organisational priority. Risk depends on context, potential impact, likelihood and the quality of available evidence. Qualitative approaches can support decisions where reliable numbers are scarce, while quantitative approaches can clarify possible financial consequences. Yet numerical outputs can imply greater certainty than their assumptions justify. Threat modelling methods such as STRIDE helped me think proactively about how systems and processes could be misused rather than only how to repair failures. I also learnt that technical controls alone cannot address weaknesses in governance, human behaviour or recovery planning.

The team project tested these ideas in a different way. Contributions arrived late, writing styles varied, and the strict word limit left little room for repetition. I felt pressure to produce a polished report while respecting colleagues’ technical arguments. Editing for a single voice risked unintentionally changing another person’s meaning, so consistency had to be balanced against accuracy and shared ownership. Word-count management was not merely a formatting task: I needed to distinguish essential analysis from material that was interesting but peripheral. This resembles cybersecurity risk prioritisation, where limited resources require defensible choices.

Checking citations also made me more aware that academic integrity is part of quality assurance. A convincing recommendation is weaker if its evidence cannot be traced. My assigned responsibility to integrate Hancock et al. (2024) prompted particular attention to the relationship between a claim and its source, although the exact reference must be checked against the final report. In retrospect, I placed too much emphasis on the final editing stage. Earlier internal deadlines, a shared style guide and incremental reference checks would have reduced last-minute risk. I also realised that coordinating a team is not simply about enforcing standards: it requires timely communication, listening and allowing colleagues to review editorial changes. Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper’s (2001) reflective approach helps me distinguish these lessons from a simple description of completed tasks.

Now what?

In future group work, I will agree internal deadlines ahead of the official submission date, maintain a shared progress tracker and introduce a common report template from the start. I will schedule an early editorial review and a separate final quality check, rather than leaving both until the deadline. I will use a reference register to verify that each significant claim is supported and that the reference list matches the citations.

Professionally, I intend to apply structured threat modelling and risk assessment to network and service scenarios, including last-mile fibre connectivity. I will practise creating risk registers, documenting assumptions, assessing the business impact of outages and identifying proportionate recovery measures. I will also develop concise, audience-appropriate reporting so technical findings can inform management decisions. These actions connect my practical telecommunications experience with the governance, analysis and communication expected of a cybersecurity professional. The module has taught me that effective security work depends on evidence, judgement, collaboration and reflection, not technical knowledge alone.

Reflection reference

Rolfe, G., Freshwater, D. and Jasper, M. (2001) Critical reflection in nursing and the helping professions: a user’s guide. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan.
