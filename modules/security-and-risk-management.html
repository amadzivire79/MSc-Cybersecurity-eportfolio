Module Overview

Understanding Security as Organisational Risk

Security and Risk Management extends cybersecurity beyond purely technical controls by examining how organisations identify, evaluate, prioritise and manage uncertainty.

The module has encouraged me to consider cybersecurity as a socio-technical discipline in which technology, organisational processes, governance, people, legal obligations and ethical responsibilities interact.

Through academic discussion, case analysis and risk-based exercises, I am developing a more structured approach to understanding threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, consequences and appropriate risk treatment.

My Approach to Cyber Risk Management

Cybersecurity risk should not be viewed simply as the presence of a vulnerability. Meaningful risk analysis requires an understanding of assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, consequences and the organisation's ability to respond.

05

Threat Analysis

Threat Modelling and Cyber-Physical Systems

My study of threat modelling reinforced the importance of understanding not only individual components but also the dependencies between physical processes, software, communication networks, sensors, controllers and users.

In complex cyber-physical environments, an apparently minor vulnerability may become significant when combined with interconnected systems or privileged pathways. Effective threat modelling therefore requires a system-level view rather than isolated examination of individual devices.

Threat modelling can help identify attack entry points by mapping trust boundaries, external interfaces, communication channels, access privileges and dependencies. This supports more systematic identification of attack paths and potential consequences.

I also learned that vulnerability prioritisation should consider scenario-specific factors. A vulnerability with moderate technical severity may warrant high priority where compromise could affect safety, operational continuity or critical infrastructure.

Cybersecurity Governance

Frameworks Supporting Risk Management

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

Provides a flexible, risk-based structure built around Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover.

Govern Identify Protect Detect Respond Recover

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

Provides a structured approach to information security management through risk assessment, risk treatment, governance and continual improvement.

NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework

Emphasises managing security risk, protecting against cyberattack, detecting security events and minimising the impact of incidents.

07

Socio-Technical Risk

Human Factors and Insider Risk

An important lesson from the module is that cyber risk is not generated exclusively by malicious attackers or technical vulnerabilities. Human behaviour, inadequate training, poor communication and procedural non-compliance can also significantly influence security outcomes.

Insider risk includes both malicious and accidental behaviour. Employees or contractors may unintentionally introduce vulnerabilities through weak configurations, inappropriate access, poor password management or failure to follow established security procedures.

This reinforced my understanding that effective risk treatment requires a combination of technological controls, governance, awareness, organisational culture and clearly defined responsibility.

08

Ethical Risk Management

Fairness, Accountability and Technology

My academic discussions on digital technologies have also highlighted that technological capability does not automatically produce better decision-making or reduced risk.

Systems may introduce new risks where organisations place excessive confidence in automated outputs, fail to consider affected stakeholders or overlook the assumptions embedded within technological solutions.

This has strengthened my view that effective risk management requires transparency, accountability, stakeholder engagement and continuous evaluation rather than reliance on technology alone.

09

Reflective Practice

My Reflection

This module is changing how I understand cybersecurity. Earlier technical work naturally encouraged me to focus primarily on vulnerabilities, systems and defensive controls. Security and Risk Management has required me to consider the broader organisational context in which those vulnerabilities exist.

I am developing a stronger appreciation of the distinction between a vulnerability and a risk. A technical weakness does not have the same significance in every environment; its importance depends upon the asset affected, potential threat actors, likelihood of exploitation and business consequences.

Threat modelling has also reinforced the importance of understanding interdependencies. Security failures can propagate across interconnected systems, meaning risk analysis must consider pathways and consequences rather than examining components in isolation.

Governance frameworks have helped me understand that effective cybersecurity requires leadership, accountability, monitoring and continual improvement. Technical controls are essential, but they must form part of a broader risk-management process.

Overall, I am becoming more confident in connecting technical security analysis with governance, organisational priorities, ethics and professional judgement.
