Module Overview

Understanding Security and Risk Management

Security and risk are increasingly interconnected within modern organisations. As enterprises adopt increasingly integrated digital systems, cybersecurity risk must be considered alongside wider operational, financial, regulatory and strategic risks.

This module develops an understanding of the methods used to identify, analyse, evaluate and treat cybersecurity risks. It considers both qualitative and quantitative risk assessment approaches and examines how different methods may be appropriate in different organisational contexts.

The module also explores established threat modelling approaches including STRIDE, DREAD, Attack Trees and PASTA, together with tools and resources that support threat analysis and vulnerability prioritisation.

Additional areas of study include security and regulatory standards, quantitative risk modelling, business continuity, disaster recovery and emerging trends within the wider field of security and risk management.

Units 3–4

Threat Modelling and Management

Units 3 and 4 develop the distinction between threats and vulnerabilities and introduce established methods for representing potential attacks and prioritising security risks.

01

STRIDE

A structured technique for identifying threats involving spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege.

02

DREAD

A risk-rating approach traditionally used to assess and prioritise the severity of identified threats.

03

Attack Trees

A graphical technique for representing an attacker's primary objective and the possible pathways through which that objective may be achieved.

04

PASTA

A risk-centred threat modelling methodology designed to connect business objectives, technical analysis and possible attack scenarios.

05

MITRE ATT&CK

A knowledge base that supports analysis of adversary tactics, techniques and behaviour during threat modelling.

06

OWASP and CVSS

Resources that assist with vulnerability understanding, threat analysis and prioritisation.

My Reflection

Threat modelling has strengthened my understanding that identifying vulnerabilities alone is not sufficient. Effective security analysis must also consider attack objectives, likely pathways, dependencies, attacker behaviour and possible consequences.

Critical Reflection and Professional Development

Final Critical Reflection

This reflection critically evaluates my learning and professional development during the twelve-unit Security and Risk Management module. Using Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper’s (2001) ‘What? So what? Now what?’ framework, I consider how academic study, collaborative assessment and practical challenges have changed my understanding of cybersecurity risk and informed my future practice.

1. WHAT? – Description of My Learning Experience

Studying Security and Risk Management has developed my understanding of how organisations identify, assess, prioritise and mitigate cybersecurity risks. Before the module, my perspective was strongly influenced by technical network operations and my work as a fibre-optic engineer. I primarily associated security with protecting network infrastructure, devices and connectivity. The module demonstrated that effective security also depends on organisational objectives, human behaviour, governance, legal obligations and business continuity.

Across twelve units, I explored qualitative and quantitative risk assessment, threat modelling, security frameworks, regulatory compliance, business continuity and disaster recovery. Activities involving STRIDE, DREAD, risk modelling and security standards introduced structured ways of identifying vulnerabilities, evaluating consequences and communicating findings. The distinction between an identified technical weakness and a risk that matters to a particular organisation became increasingly important to my thinking.

The Pampered Pets team project enabled me to apply this learning collaboratively. As Student 4, Chief Editor, Operations Manager and Visual Designer, my assigned responsibilities included drafting the introduction and conclusion, coordinating the report, maintaining the 1,000-word limit, preparing a Gantt chart and checking Harvard referencing, including Hancock et al. (2024). These responsibilities required both technical understanding and coordination with colleagues.

2. SO WHAT? – Critical Analysis of My Experience

My most significant conceptual change was recognising that cybersecurity risk management is not simply a catalogue of technical vulnerabilities. It requires judging the likelihood and consequences of threats in an organisation’s operational and strategic context. Qualitative assessments offer accessible judgements when numerical evidence is limited, whereas quantitative methods can support financial and operational decisions. Nevertheless, numerical results can create misleading confidence when assumptions are weak or data are incomplete. I therefore need to question the evidence behind a risk score, not merely accept its apparent precision.

Threat modelling challenged my earlier tendency to focus on fixing faults after they occurred. STRIDE encouraged me to anticipate how attackers might exploit systems, processes and human behaviour. However, no single framework captures every threat or replaces contextual judgement. A model is only useful when its scope, assumptions and limitations are made explicit. This insight is relevant to telecommunications, where service availability, customer equipment and operational practices can create interconnected risks.

The Pampered Pets project tested my organisational and interpersonal skills. Late contributions increased the pressure of producing a coherent report. Initially, I focused on completing my editorial tasks, but I realised that effective collaboration requires anticipating delays, agreeing expectations and reviewing progress before the deadline. Earlier internal milestones could have reduced last-minute work and given colleagues more opportunity to check the integrated document.

Integrating different writing styles was another challenge. Contributions varied in structure, terminology and depth. As editor, I needed to establish a consistent academic voice without changing colleagues’ intended meanings. This exposed a tension between quality control and shared ownership: extensive editing can improve readability but may unintentionally obscure individual arguments. In future, I would agree a common template and seek confirmation of substantive edits rather than treating editorial decisions as entirely my own.

The strict 1,000-word report limit also required difficult decisions. I initially regarded the limit as restrictive because risk management involves complex technical and organisational issues. Yet the exercise taught me to distinguish essential findings from interesting but peripheral detail. Clear prioritisation is especially important when presenting risks to senior managers, who need concise recommendations, a rationale and an understanding of residual uncertainty rather than an exhaustive technical account.

Checking Harvard citations reinforced the relationship between academic integrity and professional credibility. The requirement to incorporate Hancock et al. (2024) prompted attention to whether claims were supported and sources consistently recorded. I also learnt that leadership is not simply directing tasks: listening, negotiating changes and sharing accountability are essential when colleagues work under pressure. In retrospect, my strongest contribution was not merely polishing text but helping translate varied inputs into a usable collective report. I must still distinguish responsibilities assigned to me from outcomes evidenced by the final artefacts.

3. NOW WHAT? – Professional and Personal Development

The module has influenced how I intend to approach cybersecurity responsibilities. My telecommunications experience provides practical knowledge of infrastructure, customer connectivity and operational reliability; I now want to connect this knowledge with systematic risk identification, security governance and organisational resilience. Before recommending a technical solution, I will consider the assets at risk, relevant threat scenarios, business consequences and the suitability of proposed controls.

For future group assessments and workplace projects, I will establish responsibilities, shared writing templates and realistic milestones at the outset. I will schedule short progress reviews, maintain a decision log and introduce an interim quality check before final editing. These steps should improve transparency, reduce deadline pressure and allow all contributors to review substantive changes. I will also use a reference checklist from the beginning rather than attempting to correct citations at the end.

To develop my technical competence, I plan to practise constructing threat models, maintaining risk registers, comparing qualitative with quantitative assessments and designing business continuity scenarios. I will record the assumptions and limitations of each exercise, seek feedback and document improvements in my e-portfolio. Over the next three months, I aim to complete at least one practical threat model and one documented risk assessment linked to a telecommunications scenario, reviewing both against recognised guidance.

I will assess my progress by reviewing whether these artefacts clearly identify threats, justify risk ratings, distinguish evidence from assumptions and propose feasible mitigations. I will seek feedback on both the technical analysis and its clarity for a non-technical audience. Where weaknesses remain, I will revise the work and record what changed, making reflection an ongoing process rather than a one-off submission.

Ultimately, this module has shown me that effective cybersecurity practice integrates technical knowledge, critical judgement, ethical responsibility, collaboration and clear communication. It has strengthened my academic confidence while revealing areas that require continued practice. My next step is to turn these insights into repeatable professional habits rather than regard the completion of the module as the end of my learning.

Reference

Rolfe, G., Freshwater, D. and Jasper, M. (2001) Critical reflection in nursing and the helping professions: a user’s guide. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan.
